# Making an explainer with VisoTalk

You write one file, `doc.tsx`: a VisoTalk document (spec 0.7, a TSX module). A scene holds the narration **lines**, the **parts** (boxes of ordinary React that draw what is on screen) and an **animation** that says when each thing appears or changes, anchored to the **words** of the narration. The engine reads the skeleton (lines, parts, animations) as literals and owns time: you never write frame numbers; a picture is a function of the clock and of numbers the document tweens.

This is a creative task. You are a video designer making an explainer of top professional standard: the viewer understands the narration from what is on screen and wants to keep watching. It is finished when someone who has not read the script would be glad to publish it, not when `qa` is clean. Each scene needs a visual idea of its own subject (a metaphor, a drawing driven by the data, one object that transforms, a move of the frame) and motion that carries meaning. How it looks (palette, faces, density, the character of motion, the captions) is the work's style, below: this skill does not choose a look.

**A complete small document is in `example/`** (`doc.tsx` and `parts/`, about 4,000 characters): read it before you write your own; it shows the imports, a part per scene, the film's look in code (`parts/kit.tsx`: its look line, palette, type scale and scene frame), scenes that open on their picture, a number counted up by a tween, an SVG drawn by a tweened `draw`, a row that enters with a stagger, and entrances on words. Copy its structure, not its look.

## Setup

- The CLI is the npm package `visotalk`: run it as `npx visotalk@latest <command> doc.tsx` (keep `@latest`), or install it once with `npm i -g visotalk@latest` (in the repository: `pnpm visotalk`). Node 22.12 or later and `ffmpeg` are needed. Work files go to `.visotalk/<id>/`. Run `visotalk doctor --fix` once on a new machine (`references/setup.md`); after that, a command that needs something missing says so, so there is nothing to check first.
- Narration is Kokoro unless the document names a voice: `<Doc voice="minimax:English_Trustworthy_Man">` uses MiniMax with `MINIMAX_API_KEY` from the environment (a China account also has `MINIMAX_BASE_URL=https://api.minimaxi.com`). Never write a key into a command line or a file, and never print it.
- **Fonts**: `build` embeds, cut to the film's characters, the faces the theme's three stacks name (`sans`, `mono`, `display`; a Chinese, Japanese or Korean film gets its Noto Sans by itself) and any installed face a part names in quotes. A part sets headings with `var(--vt-display)`. `visotalk schema themes` lists the faces a film can use. The page never uses the viewer's or this machine's fonts, so never look for fonts on the system (`fc-list`, font folders). Installed: Noto Serif SC (Chinese display serif), Newsreader (Latin display serif with italic), LXGW WenKai (a kai brush face), the Noto Sans faces of each language and the built-in styles' faces (Caveat, Shantell Sans, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, Big Shoulders Display, Hanken Grotesk). These are variable fonts: any weight they cover; Inter, the Latin default, has 400 and 700.
- **Chinese, Japanese and Korean**: Kokoro speaks English only, so name a provider's voice: `<Doc lang="zh-CN" voice="minimax:Chinese (Mandarin)_News_Anchor">`. Write numbers in characters, not digits, and anchor on a phrase of two or more characters that occurs once in its line. `build` embeds the font for the characters the film uses.
- **Given a recording** (someone's voice-over or talking-head video) instead of a script: it is the narration and the clock, and `references/recorded.md` replaces steps 1 and 2 of the workflow and the pace rules.
- Other voices and providers, fonts, `render` (a video file), `play`, `export`/`import`, `build --artifact` and other languages: `references/setup.md`.

## Commands

| Command | Does | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| `validate doc.tsx` | syntax, references, anchors, and your parts' code as text (no timers, `Math.random`, `Date`, network, CSS animations); one issue per line at `doc.tsx:line:col`, most with a `fix` | free |
| `init doc.tsx --lines lines.json` | a valid skeleton from your script (`{"title", "lang", "voice", "canvas", "lines": [{"id", "text", "say", "intent"}]}`): every line in one scene `draft`, nothing drawn; starts the narration in the background | seconds |
| `prepare doc.tsx` | narration audio and word times (`voice.json`). Until it is ready, `sheet`, `still` and `qa` time each line from its words (about 2.8 a second) and say so | minutes |
| `sheet doc.tsx [--at 0.85] [--shots]` | one frame per line as one contact-sheet picture (JPEG, about 1280 wide); works from the skeleton. `--shots`: one frame per shot of a video recording instead | seconds |
| `still doc.tsx --time 12,31,47` or `--line scene/line[,…] [--at 0.85]` | several moments in one call, returned as **one** picture (2 x 2, up to four frames) with, for each moment, the sentence spoken then and what the engine measured (where the ink is, room on each side, anything past an edge or under the captions): often enough without the picture. `--zoom X,Y,W,H` shows one region of the stage at full resolution, for the smallest print | seconds |
| `qa doc.tsx` | on sampled frames: past its box or the canvas, cut off, under the captions, on screen under two seconds or never, a target no part registers, weak anchors. Overlap is not checked (layering is a normal way to show things). One line per problem, `--full` for all; exit 1 on any issue | seconds |
| `build doc.tsx --out DIR` | the finished web page, `DIR/index.html` and its files, playing from a folder (file://) or a server. It then opens the page in Chrome as a viewer does, presses Play and says whether the film ran with its narration and loaded nothing from outside the folder. It also says what the page holds: captions, the faces embedded, its files and size. Deliver it as built: do not edit, inline or re-bundle it, do not test it in a browser of your own, and do not inspect its folder | seconds |
| `asset search "<words>"` / `asset get N --id NAME` | openly licensed pictures (Wikimedia Commons, then Openverse) as text candidates; `get` saves one to `media/NAME.jpg` and prints the `<Asset>` to paste into `<Doc>` (`references/assets.md`) | seconds |
| `style list` / `style show <slug>` / `style use <slug> doc.tsx` / `style check doc.tsx` | the built-in styles: list, show, copy one into the work (only when the person names one); check the work's style | free |
| `schema [tags\|motions\|hooks\|themes]` | the vocabulary, and each theme token's value | free |

**Pictures cost context.** `sheet`, `still` and `qa` keep a ledger per document: a frame unchanged since you saw it is named, not drawn again, and past a budget of pictures (15, `VISOTALK_LOOK_BUDGET`) they print only what they measured unless `--image` asks for one more. They use Chrome one at a time: run them one after another with a tool timeout of minutes.

## The look

**When the work has `style/`** (the person's, or one copied in), read its `STYLE.md` and `style.json` before drawing and follow it; `style check doc.tsx` after changing it. When the person asks for a change to the look, decide first whether it belongs to the style or to this work alone: what any work in this look should share (the ground, colours, faces, the caption skin, how a component draws) goes into `style/`; what serves only this story (one scene's layout, a chart only it needs) goes into the work's own `parts/`. When you cannot tell, ask. Each change you make to `style/` adds one plain line to `style/CHANGES.md` saying what changed in words the person would use (`- Ground changed to newsprint`). Leave `style/previews/`, `style/poster.jpg` and `style.json`'s `slug` and `version` as they are: they are written for you. A component card names an exact `style/parts/*.tsx` file: read its literal `meta` and actual parameters before importing it, and drive a `progress` parameter from the document through its part: `<Part id="tag">{() => <NameTag name="…" show={useProp('show', 0)}/>}</Part>`, then `<Tween target="tag" to={{show: 1}} …/>` (the pack's `demo/doc.tsx` does it for each). A work's copy changes only by your edits: a newer version of the style it came from reaches it only when the person applies it. **When the person names a built-in style** or asks to see them: the packs are in `styles/<slug>/` beside this file; `style list` names them with the kinds of video each is made for and what it fits, `style show <slug>` its components, and `style use <slug> doc.tsx` copies one into the work's `style/` and writes its tokens on `<Doc>`; from then on it is the work's style. **Otherwise draw freely**: there is no style to write. Decide the look as you would for any piece of design you make, keep it in code, and make every scene from it:

- **Write the look as one line of numbers**, a comment at the top of `parts/kit.tsx`: ground (hex), ink, one accent and what it marks, the display face with its weight and size, the text face, and the medium the film is made of (paper and ink, a product's UI, a lit stage, a map). A look taken from the subject's own world holds together; an adjective ("modern", "clean") leaves the choice to a default. Everything below is that line in use.
- **The ground and the faces are the film's**, written on `<Doc tokens={{colors: {bg: …}, fonts: {sans: …, display: …}}}>` so the player paints the same ground between scenes and `build` embeds the faces; `visotalk schema themes` lists the faces and token names. A film left on the theme's grey with one sans looks unmade (`validate` warns).
- **Hierarchy you can measure**: headings in the display face at 80–120 px, four or five sizes in all, weight and colour doing the rest; nothing a viewer must read under 24 px; key text 80 px from the sides and clear of the captions below y 900.
- **One thing first in each scene**: decide what the viewer notices first and build the frame around it, at least a third of the stage; the same frame (where the heading, the key line, the figure sit) in every scene.
- **Colour carries meaning**: one accent for the focus, one meaning per colour through the film, the rest dimmed the same way each time. Atmosphere at 15–25 % opacity; less vanishes in a video.
- **Surfaces separate the figure from the ground**: panels, a shadow, a radial glow behind the focus. A full-screen linear gradient on a dark ground bands in a video: use a radial one or a flat fill.
- **Defaults to avoid unless the subject asks for them**: small things floating in the middle of a dark void; glow, neon and particles as decoration; everything centred, or fading in at once; three cards in a row; corner labels and HUD frames as ornament.

`example/parts/kit.tsx` is such a look in code (its line, palette, type scale and scene frame): copy its level of finish, not its look. What the scenes share lives in `parts/`.

## Principles

What a good video does. How to write it is in `references/`; a rule here says what must be true, not which tag makes it so.

1. **A scene opens on its picture, and the words change it.** A part is invisible until an `<Appear>`, so appear each scene's base picture `at="start"`: the heading, the panels and axes, and the chart or drawing already showing its shape: a chart, curve or drawing starts from a visible first state (low bars, a faint curve, the outline), never from nothing, and empty axes or an empty frame waiting for a word are not a picture. What the narration names is then a change inside that picture on its word: a figure that counts, a bar or curve that grows, a mark or label that arrives, a term that lights, one more element. A fact is never on screen before its word; the frame it lands in is, and the frame includes the scene's heading and key line, which name its subject. At every moment the stage above the captions is mostly in use: a frame with only a title, or only axes, is unfinished.
2. **One main change per sentence, on the word that names its subject**: the noun or the figure, not the start or the end of the sentence. A sentence that names two things is two changes on two words. The main change may carry secondary motion that serves it: a series that enters one after another (`stagger`), bars that rise in turn, the rest dimming while one lights, a figure that counts as its bar grows. Ease every motion; nothing moves at a constant speed unless it is time itself.
3. **Every object is there for a reason the narration gives**, and does not stay after the scene that needed it.
4. **Give everything time to be seen and understood: at least two seconds** from when it appears to when it goes, and the last picture of a scene stays after the narration ends. A flash is allowed only when it is the point, and the design says so.
5. **Motion means something.** An arrival says "here is a new thing", a growth says "this quantity", a slide or morph says "this became that", a draw says "this leads there". A thing that grows, moves, replaces or becomes does that on screen: an entrance that pops is not that motion. A quantity that matters is shown changing (a figure that counts, a bar that grows to its length). Motion that pulls the eye away from what the sentence is about is decoration: leave it out. A new scene's picture enters (a fade or a short rise of about half a second), it does not cut in, except the cover; it fades from the stage's ground, so the theme's `bg` is the film's ground or the change of scene flashes another colour.
6. **One focus at a time.** The base picture stays; the eye goes to what the sentence is about (it arrives, grows or lights) while the rest stays quiet. What a sentence replaces leaves, or moves into the new thing, before the new thing arrives; never show the before and after in one frame unless the sentence compares them. Things that merge go into each other without overprinting.
7. **Show the thing, not a label for it.** A sentence about a command points at the command and what it prints; about a file, at its contents; about a number, at the quantity it measures; about a law or a formula, at the formula, each term lit as it is named. For a feeling or a situation, draw a metaphor. Draw what carries the sentence; an aside stays in the narration. Where a familiar mark exists (a name, a product, a kind of thing), show it beside the name.
8. **Never what a slide deck would give.** A title, cards or chips on a grid with fade and slide entrances is page layout plus entrance animation. Each scene has a visual idea specific to its subject, and the thing the sentence is about is large enough to read at a glance; how full the frame is and how things enter are the look's, above the floor of principle 1: a sparse look puts fewer, larger things on stage from the first frame, never an empty stage waiting for a word.
9. **A pointer is easy to read.** An arrow, a bracket, a callout or a label that refers to the picture makes clear what it refers to: the line, the shape and the text are placed so that a viewer sees the connection at once. It need not touch what it explains; one that points at nothing in particular, or a label that seems to belong to nothing, confuses.
10. **A picture continues, it is not redrawn.** A picture that goes on over several sentences is one part with its numbers tweened, or the same elements moved or changed. A scene's parts end with it: a picture that goes on into the next scene is declared again there (a component, one line) and starts from its base props, or is a global part in `<Doc>`.
11. **The narration is in the lines**; the screen carries what a viewer wants to keep in view. What is said is a `<Line>` (its caption comes from it). A part draws the graphic and the text that stays useful while the scene runs: the heading, a key line of one or two short lines, the formula, the figures, the names and labels, the everyday case as a tag. Never draw a spoken sentence word for word; a label a translation must change is drawn with `useText`.
12. **Text has a scale, and is set, not typed**: a label is a phrase, a key line one or two short lines, a figure a number and a name. Use the film's type scale (the look, above), with weight, size and colour making the hierarchy.
13. **Be honest.** Never invent a measured number or claim a result nobody measured. An example the viewer is told is an example (the plausible output of a command a line names) is not a measurement: draw it. Narration is fixed audio: never state a value that depends on a param.
14. **Material is licensed and local.** Every image, clip and sound is a declared asset with its licence and source, kept in the project's library; credits are generated. Rules: `references/assets.md`.
15. **Know what a scene shows before you draw it**, sentence by sentence: what is on stage, what arrives or leaves, which word fires it. Then check the picture against that.
16. **The first frame is the cover.** Before Play the player shows the film's first frame, and a viewer judges the film by it: the title, one big picture or figure and one short line, on screen `at="start"` with no fade, and replaced by the first scene's picture a few seconds in (`references/explainers.md`).

## Workflow

Nothing refuses a step; planning, looking and judging are yours. A way that works:

1. **Gather** what the video states: facts, numbers, real output.
2. **Script** short lines, one idea each. Write numbers as words in the line (`three hundred bytes`, digits in the caption with `text="… 300 bytes"`). Then `visotalk init doc.tsx --lines lines.json`.
3. **Decide what each scene shows before you draw it**: what each sentence points at in the world (principle 7); at least two ideas that differ in kind, picking the one a newcomer would understand. Then **settle the look** (above): the work's style, the one the person named, or your own look line in `parts/kit.tsx`. Then the board: the base picture on stage from the scene's first frame (layout and sizes in px, clear of the captions below y 900), and sentence by sentence what changes in it on which word.
4. **Draw one scene, look, then plan the next**: one `<Scene>` per idea, one `<Line>` per sentence, one `<Part>` per picture, the animation once the picture exists (`references/markup.md`, `parts.md`). Keep `doc.tsx` to the skeleton and put each scene's drawing in its own file under `parts/`, imported as in `example/`: an edit then touches one small file. `still`, `sheet`, `qa` and `build` validate the document first and stop on any issue, so a separate `validate` is only for checking a change you will not look at.
5. **Look**, as a viewer who has not seen your plan: is the stage in use from the scene's first frame, could a slide deck have made it, is the thing the sentence names on screen and big enough, is anything cut off or empty by accident, does it keep the look? Redraw what fails. How to look cheaply is below.
6. `qa` for what you miss; it cannot see whether a picture matches its sentence. Deliver with `build doc.tsx --out DIR`.

### Pace and scope

- **Scope to the length asked.** Narration runs about 2.8 words a second in English and 4.5 characters a second in Chinese; a scene adds 1.2 s of lead and tail. Given a limit ("under five minutes"), write for 75 to 90 percent of it, not a fraction: four minutes is about 650 English words or 1,000 Chinese characters in eight to ten scenes, 45 seconds about 120 words in four or five.
- **Have a whole document early.** After the first scene is drawn and looked at, draw the rest simply, `validate`, `qa`, fix, within the first third of your time; then improve the weakest scenes. A complete plain document is a video; a half-polished one is not.
- **Stop `qa` on time.** At most three runs: once when whole, then after each round of fixes, fixing every problem a viewer would notice scene by scene in one edit each. Then build.
- **Look yourself, and as little as you can.** Every picture you read stays in your conversation and is sent again with every later step, so a few dozen make every step slower and dearer. Reading what `still` measured costs words; reading the picture costs far more.
  - **Measurements and `qa` first**; a picture only for what they cannot say: does it look like the thing, is it empty or crowded, can the small print be read.
  - **A round is one `sheet`, one `still` call with up to four moments you doubt (they come back as one picture, 2 x 2), and one pass of fixes; at most three rounds.** For print too small to judge in a whole frame, `still --time T --zoom X,Y,W,H` shows that region of the stage (its pixels, 1920 x 1080) at full resolution. Never cut, crop or enlarge the pictures with scripts of your own: each copy is one more picture in your conversation, outside the ledger.
  - **A second pair of eyes is optional**: when your model cannot read images, or the film is long enough that its sheets would crowd your conversation, hand the finished film once to a reviewer subagent (`agents/visotalk-look.md`, written for opencode; its text works as any harness's subagent prompt). Give it the `sheet` picture with the list `sheet` printed, and what each scene should show; it reads only those pictures and answers with a short list of problems, worst first, each with the moment and the exact change. You make the changes: it reads no code and edits nothing.

- `references/markup.md`: the syntax of `doc.tsx`, parts, targets, anchors, motions, `qa`, languages and known gotchas.
- `references/parts.md`: drawing a part: CSS layout, data-driven pictures, motion by a number, SVG, theme tokens.
- `references/assets.md`: finding, keeping and declaring material from the internet.
- `references/explainers.md`: the cover, a lesson and its formulas, reporting what a source found, series, phone screens, chapters, illustrations, the independent check.
- `references/setup.md`: setup, voices, CJK, fonts, and `render`, `play`, `export`, `build --artifact`.
- `references/recorded.md`: a person's own recording as the narration: what may not change, pauses, the presenter's place, a transparent layer for an editor.
